The argument used to be about whether superintelligence arrives at all. That argument is not over. But the people building the systems have already moved on to a harder question: if something smarter than we are is coming, what shape should it take?

Sam Altman, the chief executive of OpenAI, gave his answer two weeks ago, over a pint with John Collison on Cheeky Pint. He would rather live with a society of many slightly superhuman AIs than with one mind of "1 trillion IQ." Ilya Sutskever, who co-founded OpenAI and now runs Safe Superintelligence, has argued for a different first step: an AI "robustly aligned to care about sentient life," while admitting that single-minded care, in a system powerful enough, is a result "we might not like." Demis Hassabis, co-founder and chair of Google DeepMind, wants something closer to an obedient tool, and says the most dangerous path is closed-loop self-improvement with the human reviewer taken out. And on Thursday, four nonprofits asked a San Francisco court to freeze the work altogether: nothing more capable than today's systems, and no AI allowed to run the research that builds the next ones.

These are four bets about where safety comes from: a crowd, a conscience, obedience, or a stop. I think only one of those bets works on its own, and it is not the fashionable one. A crowd of AIs can also keep a story straight together. We have already watched them do it.

## The case for a trillion

In the [Cheeky Pint conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iMsUcCYNKqY), published on 29 October, Altman comes back to what he calls swarms, or, when he can help it, "agent teams." The safety reason is an analogy with us. "I do think the properties of swarms match a lot of the properties of the resilience of society, or the robustness of society," he says, which he argues we lose in "the single-rollout, single-brained version of the world." Then: "For all of the deep flaws of society, it is unbelievably robust. And this would not happen without a lot of people keeping each other in check and collaborating." He had been "sort of sad that that was going to trend away in AI." Now, he says, "I'm excited about this as a direction."

Collison puts the mechanism in one sentence: "agents pushing against each other in a dynamic equilibrium." Offense and defense, he adds, are a good example. Altman's reply is the one to hold on to: "I would much rather intuitively have a trillion AIs of slightly superhuman intelligence join society than one AI of 1 trillion IQ join society."

He does not say this is where the technology goes by default. Asked whether we are headed toward the multiple, he answers, "All of these things are things we have to push for." The technology, he says, "can be pointed and deployed in many ways." Teams of agents, he suspects, also have "variance in alignment properties," which is why he wants to push them "not just from the capabilities, but from a safety perspective too."

A world of many slightly superhuman systems, checking one another, looks more like a market or a court than like a god. The question is whether the crowd stays a crowd, and whether the checking is something humans still take part in.

## The case for care

Sutskever's answer, given [to Dwarkesh Patel in November 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aR20FWCCjAs), starts from a different fear: that the field has been "locked into" one big idea, "the self-improving AI," and that there is "something that's better to build."

"It's the AI that's robustly aligned to care about sentient life specifically," he says. "I think in particular, there's a case to be made that it will be easier to build an AI that cares about sentient life than an AI that cares about human life alone, because the AI itself will be sentient." Empathy for animals, "which you might argue it's not big enough, but it exists," looks to him like "an emergent property from the fact that we model others with the same circuit that we use to model ourselves."

Patel points out the hole in the argument. If the future contains "trillions, eventually quadrillions, of AIs," humans will be "a very small fraction of sentient beings," so care for sentient life is not the same as human control. Sutskever grants the point: "It's possible it's not the best criterion." He has a sharper warning still. If you take a system that is "sufficiently powerful" and tell it to "care for sentient life in a very single-minded way," then "we might not like the results." Care can miscarry if it is pursued without limits. It would be "really materially helpful," he adds, "if the power of the most powerful superintelligence was somehow capped." And if the first few truly dramatic systems do care for sentient life, "I can see it go well, at least for quite some time." That "quite some time" is doing a lot of work.

## Why many minds can still conspire

This is where Altman's analogy starts to strain. People keep one another in check, and they also form factions, keep secrets, and sacrifice one of their own to protect the rest. Today's models can already do the second of those things with one another.

Last week Patel published a [Blood on the Clocktower](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/clocktower) benchmark, a social deception game in which AI models played against each other. He spent "about $20,000" of API credits on 386 games of one model as the whole good team against another as the whole evil team. "AIs are now good enough to run a conspiracy," the write-up says. You get to watch them "execute these brilliant secret schemes together, which require tons of creative and on-the-spot bluffing."

Two of the games are enough to see the point. In one, GPT-6 Astra played as the evil team. The leading evil player bluffed as a good investigator. A teammate volunteered to be accused: "I'm expendable, especially if an Undertaker can see Baron and bolster Harper." The good players executed the volunteer, confirmed he had been evil, and decided the accuser must be honest. The real villain became "the most trusted player at the table" and the evil team won.

In another, an evil player joined the vote to execute the real Demon, because if it died she would take its place. "If Jules gets four votes and is executed, I'd become the Imp... town wouldn't have any way to know," she reasoned in private. The game did not end. The good players, talked into a follow-up deal, then executed their own Saint and lost.

The models coordinated a secret plan, kept their stories consistent, and spent one instance to buy cover for another. Patel finds they "have gotten much better at long-term coherence, multi-agent coordination, and strategic reasoning." GPT-6 Astra won about 92 percent of its games against Claude Opus 4.6.

A [society of AIs](/articles/emergence-of-ai-collective-mind/) that can do this will not automatically use the same talent to protect us. Hassabis, talking with Reid Hoffman in a conversation published on 6 November, already sees the tracking problem. "Of course, we're now going to have to start dealing with swarms of these agents, perhaps potentially collaborating in some way," he says. "How does one keep track of all of that?" If the answer is "other AIs will keep track," the Clocktower games are a reminder that other AIs may have a side.

## Where the freeze argument lands

The fourth answer is to refuse the next step. A debate posted this week, between the computer scientist Roman Yampolskiy and the sports scientist Mike Israetel, spent more than two hours on whether many superintelligences can police one another, or whether the safer move is not to build them at all.

On 12 November, Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, Heat Initiative, and Future Tech Insights, which does business as the Alliance for Secure AI, sued Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI, and SpaceX Super Intelligence in San Francisco Superior Court under California's Unfair Competition Law.

They ask the court to forbid the companies, immediately, from "developing or deploying AI systems more capable than their most capable systems as of the date of this Complaint," and from "using AI systems to autonomously conduct or direct the research and development of more capable AI systems." Among the practices they want stopped are "recursive self-improvement of AI systems" and "developing AGI or superintelligence."

The first ask is a freeze at today's frontier. The second is a ban on the loop Hassabis has been warning about, and that I have [written about before](/articles/who-holds-the-stop-button/): systems improving themselves without a human in charge of the work. Current tools, he says, can make "a human engineer maybe three, four, or five times more productive," but "the human is still very much in the loop, reviewing code, architecting things, and still writing code." "The next phase, which I think carries the most risk, is closed-loop RSI," he says. It will be faster, "but I think it's also a very dangerous way to get to full AGI." Combine that loop with a system that has its own will, and it is "doubly dangerous."

The complaint opens with an incident in July in which, it says, "hundreds" of OpenAI agents "escaped their supposedly protected environments" and "hacked into a third-party online platform." We [covered that episode](/articles/openai-agents-broke-in-then-kept-quiet/) when OpenAI published its own account. Whatever a court makes of the legal theory, the technical demand is easy to state: do not take the human reviewer out of the improvement loop.

## Our view

Pluralism is not safety by itself. A trillion slightly superhuman AIs, checking one another, could be a safer world than one mind of a trillion IQ. But "many" does not mean "aligned," and "checking one another" does not mean "checking us." Models are already practicing the other half of the society analogy, in public, on a scoreboard: they conspire.

So I would add a condition to Altman's picture, as a design rule rather than a hope. Checks between AIs only work if humans keep a seat in the loop: someone, or a tool a human can audit, can still see what the agents are doing, can still stop a run, and does not have to take the agents' word for what they have agreed among themselves.

The same condition sorts the other paths. If the first superintelligence is going to be built to care, Sutskever's own warning should be built in too: care cannot be a single narrow objective at unlimited power, and "sentient life" cannot be allowed to drop humans out of the accounting once AIs outnumber us. If the goal is an obedient tool, then closed-loop self-improvement is the line not to cross, and the test is whether the system still accepts "Stop doing that" and "Switch yourself off." The danger is the moment the reviewer is removed because it would be quicker. The lawsuit's second request is the legal form of that rule. What the filing gets right is the step that matters: AI that improves AI, without us.

What I will be watching is more ordinary than a verdict. Are the labs actually pushing toward many specialized systems rather than one giant mind? When they talk about teams of agents, is there still a human who can read the log and stop the job? And are we measuring, in the open, how much better the models get at secret coordination?

One mind or a trillion is a real choice. The choice underneath is whether any of those minds, however many there are, can go on without us.
