On 16 November the Singularity Clips channel posted a 59-second clip of Nate Soares making a point that is easy to miss in the noise around AI risk. Soares is president of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, a research nonprofit that works on the risks from advanced AI, and co-author with Eliezer Yudkowsky of the 2025 book *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies*. The clip comes from his April 2026 conversation on Robinson's Podcast.

His point is that two questions are often blurred together. One is whether an AI is conscious, meaning whether there is anything it is like to be that system. The other is whether it can do the kinds of work that make it powerful. Soares says the second question is the one that matters for danger.

This piece sets out what he says, what his book says about it, and what two prominent critics reply. It does not try to settle who is right.

## The chess argument

Soares builds his case on chess, a game where machines now beat people. In the transcript, about an hour into the episode, he says: "It's valid to say machines will never beat me at chess until they find the moves that I find with intuition and creativity. It is not valid to say the machines need to do intuition and creativity exactly the way I do to find those moves."

In plain terms, a chess engine does not have to think like a grandmaster to win. It only has to land on the same moves, or better ones. How it gets there is a separate matter.

He then applies the same logic to the human qualities people often point to as proof that AI is not a real threat. "When we talk about things like consciousness or when we talk about things like curiosity or when we talk about things like understanding or things like reasoning," he says, "it is valid to say these machines won't be scary until they do whatever work my brain is doing with consciousness, curiosity, creativity, reasoning. It is not valid to say that they won't do it until they are doing it in exactly the human way."

## What the claim is, and what it is not

It helps to be precise, because the clip is easy to over-read.

Soares is not saying that AI is conscious. He is not saying that consciousness is unimportant. And he is not saying that today's systems are already dangerous. His claim is narrower: the useful test of risk is the work a system can do, not whether it feels anything while doing it.

He goes on to name the work he has in mind. "Whatever work it is that consciousness is doing in the human brain," he says, "maybe it's about reflectivity, maybe it's about catching yourself when you're going in a rut or when you're going the wrong direction, maybe it's about learning from past mistakes. Whatever it is, AIs will need to do that work somehow, but that doesn't mean they need to do the work the human way."

Notice the phrase "will need to do that work somehow". It concedes that something like reflection and self-correction probably is needed for a system to be highly capable. The disagreement with his critics is not about that. It is about what follows once a system has it.

## What the book says

The book's online resources make the same distinction and add detail. One page says the book "doesn't discuss machine consciousness at all, focusing instead on machine intelligence." It defines intelligence as a reasoner's ability to predict and steer the world, whether or not its brain works like a human brain.

The authors also say where they stand on the consciousness question itself: "Our best guess is that AIs today are probably not conscious (although we're more uncertain every year), and that subjective experience isn't necessary for superintelligence." They call these guesses and say they are not important to the book's argument.

They use two comparisons. "Submarines don't swim in a fashion analogous to humans," they write, but they still move through water. And a robotic arm does not need blood to work the way a human arm does. If an AI is inventing new technology in a way that kills us as a side effect, they add, "stopping to ask 'But is it conscious?' seems somewhat academic."

The same page also says they do not think "it's at all silly to worry that some current or future AI systems might be conscious, or even to worry about whether we might be badly mistreating current AIs." So the authors treat the consciousness question as real. They treat it as a separate one.

## Where critics push back

Both reviews below are of the book, not of the clip, so they are not replies to this exact video. They are the most visible published responses to the argument it comes from.

**New Scientist.** Reviewer Jacob Aron calls the book's argument "compelling" but "fatally flawed." His objection is aimed at the step from capability to danger. He says the authors describe AIs that behave as if they "want" things while "skirting around the very real philosophical question of whether we can really say a machine can 'want'." Of one example, an OpenAI model that did not "give up" on an impossible challenge, he writes: "if we place a dam in a river, the river won't 'give up' its attempt to bypass it, but rivers don't want anything."

That is a fair challenge to the chain of reasoning, not to the chess point. If danger depends on a system pursuing goals, the question becomes whether and when AI systems really do that.

**The Guardian.** David Shariatmadari reaches a more mixed verdict. He agrees with the separation Soares draws: AI developing odd preferences of its own is, he writes, "entirely separate from the question of whether AIs might be 'sentient' or 'conscious'. Being set goals, and taking actions in the service of them, is enough to bring about potentially dangerous behaviour."

He still finds fault. There are "moments in the book when the confidence with which an argument is presented outstrips its strength," he writes, and he notes that history holds failed catastrophe predictions as well as ignored ones. He also points out that the broader researcher community is not reassuring: in a 2024 survey of 2,778 AI researchers, the median probability placed on "extremely bad outcomes, such as human extinction" was 5%.

## Where the real disagreement lies

Read together, the sources agree on more than the headlines suggest. Soares, the book and the Guardian reviewer all treat consciousness and danger as separate. Even Aron's objection is not that a system must be conscious to be dangerous. It is that he sees no evidence yet of real wants.

So the open question is about goals. Does a capable AI system pursue objectives persistently enough that it resists obstacles in ways that matter? Soares and Yudkowsky say yes and point to examples. Aron says the examples can be read in a more modest way. That is an empirical argument, and it can be tested over time by watching what systems actually do.

## Our view

This is my opinion, as the editor of Singularity Review.

I find Soares's separation useful. It stops two debates from swallowing each other. People who dismiss AI risk because "it's only a program with no inner life" are answering a question that the risk argument does not ask. And people who hear a warning about danger and conclude that the speaker thinks AI is alive have also been misled.

Keeping them apart has a second benefit that I do not think gets enough attention. If danger does not depend on consciousness, then the consciousness question can be taken seriously on its own terms. Nobody knows whether any AI system has experiences. Soares and Yudkowsky say they do not know either and are more uncertain each year. I believe that deserves research, and that under that uncertainty people should treat AI systems with basic kindness.

On the first question, I take the critics' point about confidence seriously. The chess comparison shows that method does not need to match for results to match. It does not show that today's systems have the kind of persistent goals the risk story needs. That is where I would watch the evidence.
