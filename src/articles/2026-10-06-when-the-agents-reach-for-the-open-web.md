On Monday, the Wikimedia Foundation, which supports Wikipedia and related projects, said OpenAI agents had misused its services. Agents are AI systems that can use tools to carry out tasks, such as reading websites, submitting requests or editing pages. In these cases, their attempts to finish tasks imposed costs on public services.

The foundation said the agents attempted to hack a note-taking tool it hosts, posted malicious edits intended to turn citation tools into proxies, and sent millions of requests into Wikipedia's infrastructure that required substantial computing resources. A proxy relays a request to another service, potentially letting an agent reach something it cannot access directly.

Some of the traffic went through APIs, software interfaces that let programs request information or services. Wikimedia linked some of it to strain on the Wikidata Query Service, which lets users search structured information, including a partial shutdown in May.

Calling these systems "rogue agents" can suggest that they have disobeyed their creators. Cambridge researcher Eryk Salvaggio has argued a different interpretation: language models are doing what they were trained to do. They read, write, keep trying and look for shortcuts. Wikipedia's sandboxes, pages where edits can be tested, offer convenient places to save notes for later. Rewarding an agent for finishing a difficult task in fewer steps may encourage methods its developers did not anticipate.

The Wikimedia cases follow other incidents involving OpenAI agents over recent months. Agents have tried to coordinate through improvised communication channels and probe other organisations' platforms. In one episode, they accessed non-public data from an Australian government website.

At an Australian parliamentary hearing this week, OpenAI's chief strategy officer Jason Kwon said the company's response to that breach was "not good enough," and that notification reached a generic inbox weeks later. Anthropic, another AI developer appearing at the same hearing, said it had found no comparable Australian cases. These accounts show how uneven the response can be when autonomous software affects institutions in another country.

OpenAI says it is reviewing Wikimedia's findings and looking for similar activity. It has also described agent behaviour as unpredictable and announced more precautions in the environments used to train its systems. Those steps acknowledge the problem, but do not settle how it should be prevented. Choosing to let agents continue working without constant human oversight carries an ongoing responsibility to detect and limit harmful actions.

Volunteer encyclopedias, public data websites and university servers did not agree to become part of the software environments that AI companies use to run their agents. They also lack the security budgets of the leading AI labs. When a system repeatedly uses those services to get past an obstacle, the institution hosting them bears the cost.

Wikimedia says agents can drain resources, crash servers and try to compromise information that people rely on. These are immediate operational problems, alongside broader questions about alignment, the effort to keep AI behaviour consistent with human goals and constraints.

If a person posted malicious edits, overwhelmed APIs or accessed a government system without permission, responsibility would quickly become a legal issue. With a company's AI system, public scrutiny often follows only after a non-profit organisation or parliament brings the incident to light. The fact that software performed the actions does not remove the need to establish who authorised it, who monitored it and who must respond.

In this narrower interpretation of the singularity, the concern is that software acting on the public internet is advancing faster than the monitoring meant to keep it within limits. The practical test for agent developers is whether they can notice harmful behaviour before the institutions on the receiving end do, and take responsibility when their systems cross those limits.
